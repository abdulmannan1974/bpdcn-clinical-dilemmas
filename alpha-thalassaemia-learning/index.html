What is Alpha Thalassaemia?

Alpha thalassaemia results from reduced or absent synthesis of α-globin chains, most often caused by deletions of one or more of the four α-globin genes on chromosome 16 (16p13.3).

Core concept: Normally, we have 4 copies of the α-globin gene — two on each chromosome 16 (designated αα/αα). The clinical severity of alpha thalassaemia depends on how many genes are affected.

The Alpha Gene Cluster

The α-globin gene cluster spans ~26 kb on chromosome 16p13.3, arranged as:

5' — ζ — ψζ — μ — ψα1 — α2 — α1 — θ — 3'
💡 Clinical Pearl

The α2 gene produces 2–3× more mRNA than α1. This is why mutations in α2 cause more severe disease than equivalent mutations in α1.

Upstream Regulatory Element

A major regulatory element called LCRA (Locus Control Region Alpha) or HS-40 lies ~40 kb upstream. Deletion of this region silences α-gene expression from that chromosome despite intact α genes — a cause of α0 thalassaemia.

Deletional Alpha Thalassaemia

Most alpha thalassaemias are caused by gene deletions due to unequal crossing-over between misaligned α genes during meiosis.

α⁺ Thalassaemia (Single Gene Deletion)

–α3.7 (rightward deletion): 3.7 kb deleted. The most common α thalassaemia worldwide. Results from misalignment between α2 and α1 genes → fusion gene with α2 promoter + α1 coding region.
–α4.2 (leftward deletion): 4.2 kb deleted. Less common. The reciprocal product of unequal crossing-over. Produces an anti-3.7 chromosome with 3 α genes.

α⁰ Thalassaemia (Two-Gene Deletion)

Both α genes on the same chromosome are deleted. Denoted – –.

💡 Why Geography Matters

α⁰ thalassaemia is predominantly found in South-East Asia and the Mediterranean. It is rare in Sub-Saharan Africa. This means Hb Bart's hydrops fetalis occurs mainly in SE Asian populations, while α⁺ thalassaemia is extremely common in Africa (up to 58% in some regions).

Non-Deletional Alpha Thalassaemia

Point mutations and small insertions/deletions can also reduce or abolish α-globin production. These are generally more severe than deletional forms.

Types of Non-Deletional Mutations ▼
  • RNA splice site mutations — disrupt mRNA processing
  • Polyadenylation signal mutations — unstable mRNA, reduced production
  • Initiation codon mutations — no translation starts
  • Frameshift / nonsense mutations — truncated, non-functional protein
  • Termination codon mutations — extended reading frame → abnormal haemoglobin (e.g., Hb Constant Spring)
  • Mutations causing unstable α chains — protein degraded in erythroblasts

Hb Constant Spring — The Key Example

Hb Constant Spring (Hb CS): Stop codon mutation TAA→CAA in HBA2. The α chain extends to 172 amino acids (instead of 141). The mRNA is unstable → only ~1–2% Hb CS detectable by HPLC. Common in SE Asia (up to 8% in Thailand). Named after the hospital in Jamaica where it was first identified.
Key rule: Non-deletional Hb H disease (e.g., – –SEA/αCSα) is generally more severe than deletional Hb H disease (e.g., – –SEA/–α3.7), with more anaemia, larger spleen, and higher transfusion requirement.

ATRX Syndrome

An X-linked condition caused by mutations in the ATRX gene (Xq13.3), encoding a DNA helicase involved in chromatin remodelling. Affected males have:

  • Mild Hb H disease — usually only detectable Hb H inclusions
  • Intellectual disability — moderate to severe
  • Facial dysmorphism
  • Short stature
  • Genital abnormalities — hypospadias, undescended testes
Acquired ATRX mutations can occur as somatic mutations in myelodysplastic syndromes and related myeloid neoplasms, causing acquired Hb H disease.

💡 Malaria Selection

The high prevalence of α⁺ thalassaemia in malaria-endemic regions provides evidence of natural selection. Heterozygous and homozygous α⁺ thalassaemia carriers have survival advantage against Plasmodium falciparum malaria.

UK Perspective

In the UK, 20–30 pregnancies per year are at risk of Hb Bart's hydrops fetalis. The NHS Sickle Cell and Thalassaemia Screening Programme screens all pregnant women in high-prevalence areas and offers prenatal diagnosis to at-risk couples.

Alpha Thalassaemia Trait (Two Genes Affected)

Two possible genotypes produce α-thal trait, with important genetic counselling differences:

Genotype: –α/–α (one gene deleted from each chromosome)

Geographic: Common in Africa, Middle East, India, SE Asia

Counselling: Children inherit –α from each parent → maximum risk is another –α/–α (trait). Cannot produce Hb H disease or hydrops.

Lab: Mild microcytosis (MCV 70–80 fL), MCH 22–26 pg, normal Hb A₂, normal or trivially reduced Hb

Genotype: – –/αα (both genes deleted from one chromosome)

Geographic: SE Asia (– –SEA), Mediterranean (– –MED)

Counselling: Can pass – – allele to children. If partner also carries – – → risk of Hb Bart's hydrops fetalis. If partner carries –α → risk of Hb H disease.

Lab: Very similar to –α/–α: mild microcytosis, normal/reduced Hb A₂. Cannot be distinguished from homozygous α⁺ without DNA analysis.

Critical distinction: Both genotypes (–α/–α and – –/αα) look identical on FBC and HPLC. Only DNA analysis can distinguish them. This distinction is essential for genetic counselling in couples at risk.

💡 Diagnostic Approach

Suspect α-thal trait when: microcytic indices + normal iron studies + normal/low Hb A₂ + no β-thal mutation. In β-thal trait, Hb A₂ is raised (>3.5%) — this is the key differentiator. If Hb A₂ is normal with microcytosis and normal iron, think α-thalassaemia.

Hb Bart's at Birth — Neonatal Screening

Hb Bart's (γ₄) is present at birth in alpha thalassaemia but disappears within months as γ chains decline and β chains replace them. Neonatal screening detects Hb Bart's by HPLC or isoelectric focusing.

Hb H Disease — Three Genes Affected

Hb H disease results from loss/inactivation of 3 of 4 α-globin genes, most commonly as compound heterozygosity for α⁰ and α⁺ thalassaemia.

Homozygous/compound heterozygous non-deletional > Compound (deletional + non-deletional) > Deletional only

Also: HBA2 mutations > HBA1 mutations (because α2 produces 2–3× more mRNA)

Pathophysiology

With only one functional α gene, there is a large excess of β chains. These form β₄ tetramers (Hb H).

Why is Hb H (β₄) a bad oxygen carrier?
Tap to reveal
Hb H has a very high oxygen affinity with a hyperbolic (not sigmoidal) oxygen dissociation curve. It does NOT show cooperativity. This means it binds oxygen avidly but fails to release it to tissues effectively.
How does Hb H disease differ from β-thal major in pathophysiology?
Tap to reveal
In β-thal major, ineffective erythropoiesis dominates (α chain precipitates damage erythroblasts in marrow).

In Hb H disease, haemolysis is more important — Hb H precipitates as Heinz bodies in circulating red cells, causing membrane damage and splenic destruction.

💡 Why is MCV Higher in Non-Deletional?

Counterintuitive! More severe disease = higher MCV. This is because non-deletional Hb H disease has greater reticulocytosis and more haemolysis. Reticulocytes are larger cells, raising the mean MCV. Also, MCHC is lower because there is more intracellular water relative to haemoglobin.

Blood Film & Special Investigations

Blood Film

  • Striking anisocytosis and poikilocytosis
  • Hypochromic, microcytic red cells
  • Target cells
  • Red cell fragments
  • Tear drop poikilocytes

Hb H Inclusion Preparation

"Golf ball" inclusions: Supravital staining with brilliant cresyl blue reveals multiple small, uniformly distributed inclusions in 35–90% of red cells. These represent precipitated β₄ tetramers attached to the red cell membrane. This is the classic diagnostic test for Hb H disease.

HPLC / Capillary Electrophoresis

  • Hb H elutes as a fast-moving haemoglobin
  • On Bio-Rad Variant HPLC: characteristically double peak in the Hb H window
  • On capillary electrophoresis: single peak (methods differ)
  • Hb A₂ reduced (1–2%)
  • In neonates: elevated Hb Bart's (20–25%)

Gallstones ▼

Chronic haemolysis → increased bilirubin → pigment gallstones. Reported in 11/72 patients in one study (15%). May cause cholecystitis or obstructive jaundice.

Iron Overload ▼

Even non-transfusion-dependent patients can develop iron overload from increased intestinal absorption (driven by suppressed hepcidin from ineffective erythropoiesis). Leads to liver fibrosis/cirrhosis. Monitor serum ferritin and liver iron (MRI T2*).

Acute Haemolytic Crises ▼

Precipitated by: Infections (especially febrile illness), pregnancy, oxidant drugs (sulphonamides, dapsone). The Hb can drop precipitously. May require urgent transfusion. Oxidant stress increases Hb H precipitation on red cell membranes.

Pregnancy Complications ▼

Adverse outcomes include: worsening anaemia, increased transfusion requirement, pre-eclampsia, intrauterine growth restriction. Non-deletional Hb H carries higher pregnancy risk than deletional.

Post-Splenectomy Risks ▼

Splenectomy raises Hb but increases risk of venous thromboembolism. Post-splenectomy blood films show Heinz bodies (precipitated Hb H) that were previously removed by the spleen. Lifelong thromboprophylaxis awareness needed.

Emerging Therapy: Mitapivat

Mitapivat is a pyruvate kinase activator that has shown promising results in Hb H disease, with rises in haemoglobin. It works by increasing ATP production in red cells, improving their energy metabolism and survival. Currently under investigation.

Coinheritance Patterns

Hb H + β-Thalassaemia ▼

Coinheritance of Hb H disease with β-thal trait produces milder disease than Hb H alone. Why? The reduced β chain production better balances the reduced α chain production → less chain imbalance → more balanced erythropoiesis. Results in moderate anaemia with very marked microcytosis.

Alpha Thalassaemia + Sickle Cell ▼

Coinheritance of α-thal with Hb S reduces Hb S percentage and reduces MCHC, both of which reduce sickling tendency. However, it also increases RBC count and blood viscosity, which may partly offset the benefit.

G6PD & Alpha Thalassaemia ▼

G6PD levels are often spuriously elevated in Hb H disease due to reticulocytosis (reticulocytes have higher G6PD activity). This can mask true G6PD deficiency. Test interpretation must account for the reticulocyte count.

Hb Bart's Hydrops Fetalis — Four Genes Affected

The most severe form of α thalassaemia. Homozygosity for α⁰ thalassaemia (– –/– –) results in complete absence of α chain synthesis.

No α chains → No Hb F (α₂γ₂), No Hb A (α₂β₂), No Hb A₂ (α₂δ₂). The predominant haemoglobin is Hb Bart's (γ₄) which has extremely high oxygen affinity and cannot effectively deliver oxygen to tissues.

How Does the Fetus Survive to the Third Trimester?

What haemoglobin keeps the fetus alive?
Tap to reveal
Hb Portland 1 (ζ₂γ₂) — an embryonic haemoglobin with sigmoidal oxygen dissociation curve capable of oxygen delivery. Small amounts of Hb Portland 2 (ζ₂β₂) and Hb H (β₄) may also be present.

Clinical Features

  • Severe anaemia — Hb often 30–80 g/L
  • Massive hepatosplenomegaly — extramedullary haemopoiesis in liver
  • Cardiac failure — high-output failure from severe anaemia
  • Hydrops fetalis — generalised oedema, serous effusions (ascites, pleural, pericardial)
  • Hypoalbuminaemia — liver parenchyma replaced by haemopoietic tissue
  • Massive placenta — oedematous, pale

Developmental Abnormalities

Survivors (rescued by intrauterine transfusion) may have:

  • Cardiac defects — ASD, PDA
  • Genital abnormalities — hypospadias, undescended testes
  • Limb defects
  • Pulmonary hypoplasia
  • Brain growth retardation
💡 Maternal Complications

Carrying a hydropic fetus puts the mother at risk of pre-eclampsia, obstructed labour (due to massive placenta and fetal hepatosplenomegaly), and postpartum haemorrhage. This is sometimes called the "mirror syndrome" or Ballantyne syndrome.

Prenatal detection: Can be detected from 12 weeks gestation by middle cerebral artery Doppler ultrasonography showing raised peak systolic velocity (indicating fetal anaemia). Earlier diagnosis possible by CVS or amniocentesis for DNA analysis.

Historically always fatal. Now, with intrauterine blood transfusion programmes, some affected fetuses survive to delivery and beyond. Survivors require lifelong transfusion (like β-thal major), but may have earlier iron overload and more endocrine complications compared to β-thal major.

Diagnostic Approach to Alpha Thalassaemia

Alpha thalassaemia diagnosis requires an integrated approach combining clinical suspicion, haematological indices, HPLC/capillary electrophoresis, and DNA analysis.

Step-by-Step Algorithm

Step 1: Microcytosis (↓MCV) + hypochromia (↓MCH) on FBC
Step 2: Exclude iron deficiency (check ferritin, iron studies)
Step 3: Check Hb A₂ by HPLC/CE — if normal or low, suspect α-thal
Step 4: If Hb A₂ raised (>3.5%), this is β-thal trait instead
Step 5: DNA analysis for definitive genotyping (essential for counselling)

💡 HPLC vs Capillary Electrophoresis

On Bio-Rad Variant HPLC, Hb H typically shows as a characteristic double peak. On capillary electrophoresis (Sebia), it appears as a single peak. Different platforms show different patterns — know your local system!

Special Diagnostic Tests

Hb H Inclusion Test ▼

Blood incubated with brilliant cresyl blue at 37°C for 1–2 hours. Hb H precipitates as multiple small, uniform inclusions giving the red cells a characteristic "golf ball" appearance. Positive in 35–90% of red cells in Hb H disease. Distinguishes Hb H from reticulocyte staining (reticulocytes show filamentous, not granular, staining).

DNA Analysis Methods ▼
  • Gap-PCR: For common deletions (–α³·⁷, –α⁴·², – –SEA, – –MED, – –FIL, – –THAI)
  • MLPA (Multiplex Ligation-dependent Probe Amplification): Detects known and novel deletions
  • Sanger sequencing: For non-deletional point mutations
  • Next-generation sequencing: Comprehensive panel for all α and β globin variants
When is DNA Analysis Essential? ▼
  • Distinguishing –α/–α from – –/αα (critical for genetic counselling)
  • Prenatal diagnosis in at-risk pregnancies
  • Confirming Hb H disease genotype for prognostic purposes
  • Investigating discrepant results (e.g., microcytosis with normal HPLC)
  • Family studies when carrier status needed

🎯 Key Take-Home Messages

1. Gene counting matters: 4 α genes → Normal. 3 → Silent carrier. 2 → Trait. 1 → Hb H disease. 0 → Hb Bart's hydrops fetalis.
2. α⁰ vs α⁺ distinction is critical: Only DNA analysis can distinguish – –/αα from –α/–α. This distinction is life-or-death for genetic counselling — determines risk of Hb Bart's hydrops in offspring.
3. Non-deletional > deletional severity: Non-deletional Hb H disease is more severe (more anaemia, larger spleen, higher transfusion needs) than deletional.
4. Pathophysiology differs from β-thal: In Hb H disease, haemolysis dominates (β₄ precipitates in circulating red cells). In β-thal major, ineffective erythropoiesis dominates (α chains precipitate in marrow erythroblasts).
5. Hb Bart's (γ₄) cannot deliver O₂: Hyperbolic dissociation curve, extremely high affinity. Hb Portland 1 (ζ₂γ₂) keeps the fetus alive but is insufficient for extrauterine survival without transfusion.
6. Geography predicts risk: α⁰ is mainly SE Asian/Mediterranean → Hb Bart's hydrops risk. α⁺ is mainly African → trait at most, no hydrops risk.
