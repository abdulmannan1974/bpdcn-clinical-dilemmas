🟣 Diagnosis · First Presentation
"Bruise-like patches" in a 70-year-old man — what's in the differential?
▾
📋 Clinical Vignette
A 70-year-old man presents with a 6-week history of multiple asymptomatic bruise-coloured skin plaques on the trunk and limbs. He has mild splenomegaly. FBC shows pancytopenia: Hb 9.2, WBC 3.1, Plt 68. Blood film shows occasional circulating large atypical cells. There is no history of lymphoma or leukaemia.

Skin biopsy: a dense monotonous blastic infiltrate in the dermis with grenz zone preservation. Cells are medium-large with finely dispersed chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli. Mitoses are frequent.
Q1. Which immunohistochemistry panel is most diagnostically critical at this stage?
✓ Expert Explanation
The clinical scenario — elderly male, bruise-like plaques, blastic dermal infiltrate — is the quintessential BPDCN presentation. 70–90% of BPDCN patients present with skin lesions, often as the first and only finding at diagnosis. Table 38.2

The diagnostic panel must simultaneously confirm BPDCN-specific markers and exclude lineage-defining antigens. Per ICC 2022, ≥2 of the following must be positive: TCF4, CD123, CD303, TCL1, CD304. CD56 supports the diagnosis but is not required. Critically, CD3, CD20/CD79a, and MPO must be negative to exclude T-cell, B-cell, and myeloid neoplasms respectively.
Q2. Immunostaining returns: CD4+, CD123 (bright)+, TCL1+, TCF4+, CD303+, CD56−, CD3−, MPO−, CD20−. How many ICC diagnostic criteria are satisfied?
✓ Expert Explanation
Four ICC diagnostic criteria are satisfied: TCF4, CD123, CD303, and TCL1. The threshold for diagnosis is ≥2, so this case is a definitive BPDCN. CD56 negativity, while somewhat less typical (~75% are positive), does not exclude the diagnosis. Table 38.2

TCF4 (E2-2) is arguably the most specific single marker — it is a transcription factor that drives pDC differentiation and is expressed in essentially all BPDCN cases. CD123 (IL-3 receptor α-chain) is both diagnostic and the target of tagraxofusp.
★ Key Teaching Points
BPDCN accounts for <0.5% of all haematological malignancies — median age 67, M:F ratio 3–4:1
70–90% of patients have skin lesions at diagnosis, often as the sentinel finding; 50–90% have bone marrow involvement
ICC 2022 requires ≥2 of: TCF4, CD123, CD303 (BDCA-2), TCL1, CD304 (BDCA-4)
The grenz zone (sparing of papillary dermis) is a helpful but not constant morphological clue

⚠️ Diagnostic Trap · AML vs BPDCN
CD4+CD123+ blasts in bone marrow — is this AML or BPDCN?
▾
📋 Clinical Vignette
A 65-year-old man is referred for pancytopenia. No skin lesions. Bone marrow biopsy shows 45% blasts which are medium-sized with oval nuclei and finely dispersed chromatin. Flow cytometry: CD4+ (dim), CD123+, CD56+ (partial), HLA-DR+. MPO−, CD3−, CD19−. NGS panel: TET2 (VAF 45%), ASXL1 (VAF 38%), no NPM1, FLT3-ITD, CEBPA.

The referring haematologist has provisionally diagnosed AML, NOS.
Q1. What critical additional staining is needed before accepting an AML diagnosis?
✓ Expert Explanation
This is a classic BPDCN diagnostic trap. CD4+/CD123+/MPO− blasts must never be reflexively called AML without completing the BPDCN-specific panel. TCF4, TCL1, CD303, and CD304 are the defining markers that distinguish BPDCN from CD4+CD123+ AML, monocytic or otherwise.

TET2 and ASXL1 mutations are not discriminatory — they occur in both BPDCN (TET2: 36–80%, ASXL1: 31–44%) and myeloid neoplasms, as both may arise from the same mutant haematopoietic stem cell clone. These mutations reflect a clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) background rather than lineage identity. Fig 38.4
Q2. TCF4 and TCL1 return strongly positive, CD303 positive. The diagnosis is now confirmed as BPDCN. How do TET2/ASXL1 mutations fit into this picture?
✓ Expert Explanation
Approximately 20–30% of BPDCN cases are accompanied by a concurrent myeloid neoplasm (MDS, CMML, or AML), reflecting their shared origin from a mutant multipotent progenitor. TET2 and ASXL1 are "trunk" mutations present in the ancestral clone that can give rise to both myeloid differentiation and pDC differentiation. Genetics section

This does not change the BPDCN classification, but clinically it means: always assess the bone marrow for a concurrent myeloid neoplasm, as this impacts post-BPDCN treatment planning (residual MDS may progress after BPDCN therapy).
★ Key Teaching Points
CD4+/CD123+/MPO− blast populations should ALWAYS have TCF4, TCL1, CD303, and CD304 staining to exclude BPDCN
TET2 (36–80%) and ASXL1 (31–44%) are the most common mutations in BPDCN — identical to CHIP/myeloid mutations, so lineage-specific markers are essential
20–30% of BPDCN cases have a co-existing myeloid neoplasm (MDS, CMML, AML) — look actively for this
BPDCN is immunophenotypically distinct from plasmacytoid monocytes seen in CMML (the latter lack TCF4 and TCL1)

💊 Treatment Decision · First-Line Therapy
Newly diagnosed BPDCN in a fit 58-year-old — what do you treat with?
▾
📋 Clinical Vignette
A 58-year-old woman — unusually, female BPDCN — presents with generalised skin plaques and lymphadenopathy. Bone marrow: 30% BPDCN infiltration. No CNS involvement on CSF examination. ECOG 0, no significant comorbidities. CD123 bright by flow. No concurrent myeloid neoplasm.

IHC: TCF4+, CD123 (bright)+, CD303+, TCL1+, CD56+. NGS: TET2, ASXL1 mutations only. Conventional karyotype: complex (3 abnormalities). She has an 8/10 HLA-matched unrelated donor available.
Q1. What is the preferred first-line treatment strategy for this fit patient?
✓ Expert Explanation
Tagraxofusp-erzs (Elzonris, SL-401) is a CD123-targeted recombinant fusion protein consisting of IL-3 fused to truncated diphtheria toxin. It binds CD123 on BPDCN cells with extraordinary affinity, internalises, and delivers cytotoxic diphtheria toxin intracellularly. It received FDA approval for BPDCN in December 2018 — the first drug specifically approved for this disease.

The pivotal trial (STML-401-0114) demonstrated a 90% overall response rate in treatment-naïve patients. The standard pathway is: tagraxofusp induction → remission → allogeneic SCT consolidation for eligible patients. Without SCT, median OS remains poor (9–24 months), but SCT offers the possibility of long-term disease control. Treatment section
Q2. What is the most important safety monitoring required during tagraxofusp infusion?
✓ Expert Explanation
Capillary leak syndrome (CLS) is the principal and potentially fatal toxicity of tagraxofusp. The mechanism involves cytokine release from off-target IL-3 receptor signalling in endothelial cells. CLS manifests as rapid weight gain, hypoalbuminaemia, hypotension, and pulmonary oedema.

Monitoring protocol: daily weight, serum albumin before each dose, blood pressure. A serum albumin <3.2 g/dL before a dose or rapid weight gain (≥1.5 kg) warrants dose delay. Dexamethasone pre-medication reduces CLS risk. This toxicity is why tagraxofusp must be administered in a hospital or clinic setting with resuscitation facilities available.
★ Key Teaching Points
Tagraxofusp is the only FDA-approved drug specifically for BPDCN — CD123 (IL-3Rα) targeting with diphtheria toxin fusion protein
Capillary leak syndrome is the dose-limiting toxicity of tagraxofusp — monitor daily weight and albumin during treatment
Allogeneic SCT consolidation remains the only potentially curative strategy; without it, relapse is almost universal
Venetoclax has emerging evidence in BPDCN due to BCL2 overexpression, but is not yet standard first-line monotherapy

🧠 Staging · CNS Assessment
BPDCN in remission — should CSF assessment and CNS prophylaxis be routine?
▾
📋 Clinical Vignette
The same 58-year-old patient achieves complete response after tagraxofusp cycle 2. Pre-transplant staging PET-CT is clear. She has no neurological symptoms. The transplant coordinator asks whether CNS assessment is necessary before proceeding to allo-SCT, and whether IT chemotherapy prophylaxis should be given.
Q1. Is CNS staging (lumbar puncture / CSF flow cytometry) necessary in BPDCN?
✓ Expert Explanation
CNS involvement occurs in ~10–30% of BPDCN cases and is a major cause of treatment failure and relapse. The CNS tropism of BPDCN mirrors its normal pDC counterpart — plasmacytoid dendritic cells naturally traffic to the meninges. This is analogous to CNS involvement in Burkitt lymphoma or ALL, not typical solid tumour brain metastasis.

Critically, CNS involvement can be cytologically occult — flow cytometry of CSF is far more sensitive than cytology alone. Standard practice therefore includes: CSF examination with flow cytometry at diagnosis, pre-SCT, and any time CNS relapse is suspected. CNS-directed prophylaxis (IT methotrexate ± cytarabine) is recommended in most guidelines. Clinical features section
Q2. Pre-SCT CSF shows 3 cells/µL with flow cytometry detecting 0.4% CD4+/CD123+/TCF4+ events. Is this CNS disease?
✓ Expert Explanation
Immunophenotypically confirmed BPDCN cells in the CSF — even at very low percentages — constitutes CNS involvement and should be treated. The presence of CD4+/CD123+/TCF4+ cells with the BPDCN immunophenotype by flow cytometry is definitive, regardless of absolute cell count.

Management implications: intrathecal chemotherapy (methotrexate ± cytarabine ± hydrocortisone) should be given until CSF clears, followed by CNS-directed consolidation. This CNS involvement also informs conditioning regimen choice for SCT (TBI-containing regimens may offer better CNS penetration). The transplant should not be deferred simply for CNS involvement, but the CSF should be cleared first.
★ Key Teaching Points
CNS tropism is a defining biological feature of BPDCN — 10–30% of cases involve the CNS
CSF flow cytometry is far more sensitive than cytology and should always be performed in staging and pre-SCT assessment
CNS involvement does not preclude allo-SCT but must be treated with IT chemotherapy to achieve CSF clearance first
CNS relapse post-SCT is a recognised pattern — surveillance CSF sampling during follow-up is justified

👦 Paediatric vs Adult · Molecular Biology
BPDCN in a 12-year-old boy — is this the same disease?
▾
📋 Clinical Vignette
A 12-year-old boy presents with skin nodules, lymphadenopathy and bone marrow infiltration. Skin biopsy confirms BPDCN (TCF4+, CD123+, CD303+, TCL1+). NGS panel: MYB rearrangement detected. No TET2 or ASXL1 mutations. Conventional karyotype: normal diploid (no complex cytogenetics).

Parents ask whether the genetic findings in their son are the same as "typical" BPDCN they've read about.
Q1. What does the MYB rearrangement tell us about this paediatric BPDCN compared to adult cases?
✓ Expert Explanation
Paediatric BPDCN is genetically distinct from the adult disease. MYB rearrangements are enriched in paediatric BPDCN, occur in the absence of TET2/ASXL1 clonal haematopoiesis mutations (which are age-related CHIP phenomena), and are associated with a simpler karyotype — no complex cytogenetic abnormalities. Genetics section

This reflects fundamentally different pathogenesis: adult BPDCN arises on a background of age-related clonal haematopoiesis, whereas paediatric cases appear to arise de novo with specific transcriptional driver alterations. The male predominance persists across all ages (linked to ZRSR2 mutations on the X chromosome in adults), but the molecular landscape differs significantly.
Q2. What molecular finding in BPDCN is associated with the most aggressive clinical behaviour?
✓ Expert Explanation
MYC rearrangements define a particularly aggressive subset of BPDCN characterised by large cell or immunoblastic morphology — sometimes called the "large cell" variant of BPDCN. These cases show frank large cells with prominent nucleoli, mimicking diffuse large B-cell lymphoma morphologically. Genetics section

The clinical course with MYC-rearranged BPDCN is extremely aggressive and outcomes are particularly poor even with intensive therapy. Recognising this subtype is important as it may warrant consideration of more intensive regimens and earlier transplant referral if response is achieved. MYC FISH should be considered in cases with large cell morphology.
★ Key Teaching Points
Paediatric BPDCN is enriched for MYB rearrangements and lacks age-related TET2/ASXL1 CHIP mutations — a genetically distinct entity
ZRSR2 mutations (X-linked splicing factor) explain the striking male predominance seen across all ages of BPDCN
MYC-rearranged BPDCN shows large cell/immunoblastic morphology — the most aggressive subtype with poorest outcomes
Complex karyotype is characteristic of typical adult BPDCN (absent in most paediatric cases), associated with CHIP background

🔬 Morphology · Diagnostic Spectrum
Tadpole cells and unusual morphology — recognising BPDCN variants
▾
📋 Clinical Vignette
A trainee haematologist reviews a blood film from a 72-year-old patient with skin lesions and circulating atypical cells. She notes cells with elongated cytoplasmic projections resembling "tadpoles" and cells with granular pale cytoplasm. Some cells have an irregular, convoluted nuclear contour. She is uncertain whether this is monocytic AML, T-lymphoma, or something else.

A colleague suggests: "These could be BPDCN cells."
Q1. What is the morphological spectrum of BPDCN, and is there a pathognomonic feature?
✓ Expert Explanation
BPDCN shows considerable morphological variability. The "classic" form features medium-sized blasts with oval or irregular nuclei, finely dispersed (blastic) chromatin, and 1–2 small nucleoli. A variable proportion have pale/granular cytoplasm mimicking monocytes. Morphology section

"Tadpole cells" (cells with elongated cytoplasmic projections) are a recognised but non-specific cytological curiosity in BPDCN. Some cases show frank large cell/immunoblastic morphology (MYC-rearranged). A small proportion resemble small lymphoid cells. This heterogeneity is precisely why morphology alone is insufficient and immunophenotyping is mandated for all suspected cases — no morphological feature is pathognomonic.
Q2. Which immunophenotypic marker combination best separates BPDCN from AML-M5 (monocytic AML)?
Quick reference — immunophenotype comparison:
BPDCN CD4+ TCF4+ CD123+ CD303+ TCL1+ MPO−
AML-M5 CD4+ TCF4− CD123 (variable) CD303− TCL1− MPO−/weak
✓ Expert Explanation
TCF4 (E2-2) is the most discriminating marker: it is the master transcription factor for pDC differentiation and is expressed in virtually all BPDCN but absent in monocytic AML. TCL1 is similarly absent from myeloid lineage cells.

CD4 and CD123 are shared between BPDCN and monocytic AML (both can be CD4+/CD123+/MPO−), which is exactly why the original "AML" call in Case 2 was erroneous. CD56 is positive in only ~75% of BPDCN and can be seen in other myeloid neoplasms. CD14 absence helps but is not definitive. The take-home: TCF4 and TCL1 are the deal-breakers in this differential.
★ Key Teaching Points
BPDCN morphology is variable — blastic, large cell (MYC-rearranged), and small lymphoid variants all exist; "tadpole cells" are a clue but not diagnostic
TCF4 (E2-2) is the most specific single marker: it is the master pDC transcription factor, absent in all myeloid lineage cells
CD4+/CD123+/MPO− is shared between BPDCN and monocytic AML — TCF4 and TCL1 are the discriminating markers
The grenz zone in skin biopsies (spared superficial papillary dermis) is a helpful but inconstant morphological clue

🔄 Relapsed/Refractory · Salvage Options
BPDCN relapse post-SCT in an elderly patient — venetoclax and beyond
▾
📋 Clinical Vignette
A 73-year-old man with BPDCN who achieved CR with tagraxofusp and underwent allo-SCT (RIC conditioning, MUD) 18 months ago. He now presents with recurrent skin lesions. Repeat biopsy confirms BPDCN relapse (same immunophenotype including CD123+, BCL2+). ECOG performance status 2. He is not fit for further aggressive chemotherapy. The transplant team confirms he is not suitable for DLI due to poor performance status.
Q1. What is the most rational targeted salvage option for this patient, and what is the biological rationale?
✓ Expert Explanation
Venetoclax (BCL2 inhibitor) has rational use in BPDCN because BPDCN cells consistently overexpress BCL2 as part of their pDC lineage programme. BCL2 is required for pDC survival in normal biology, and BPDCN cells retain and amplify this BCL2 dependence. Case series and early clinical data support venetoclax ± azacitidine activity in R/R BPDCN. Treatment section

Re-challenge with tagraxofusp is sometimes attempted but carries risk of CLS in patients with prior exposure and is not standard. CD20 is absent from BPDCN (it is used to exclude B-cell lymphoma). CD30 expression is not a feature of BPDCN. CAR-T targeting CD123 is in clinical trials and represents an emerging option.
Q2. What is the overall prognosis of BPDCN, and which patient group achieves long-term survival?
✓ Expert Explanation
The median overall survival for BPDCN is 9–24 months in most series, reflecting the aggressive natural history and high relapse rate even after complete remission induction. Prognosis section

The best long-term outcomes — with potential cure — are seen in patients who: (1) achieve CR with tagraxofusp or intensive induction, (2) proceed to allogeneic SCT in first remission, and (3) maintain durable engraftment. 5-year OS post-allo-SCT in CR1 ranges 30–50% in retrospective series. Elderly patients, those with concurrent myeloid neoplasms, or those who relapse post-SCT have very poor outcomes. Skin-limited disease does not reliably predict better prognosis — systemic staging always reveals dissemination.
★ Key Teaching Points
Venetoclax exploits BCL2 overexpression — a conserved feature from the normal pDC biology retained in BPDCN
Median OS 9–24 months overall; allo-SCT in CR1 offers the best chance of long-term disease control (30–50% 5-year OS)
BPDCN is essentially never cured without allo-SCT consolidation in eligible patients — relapse is near-universal with chemotherapy alone
CD123-targeting CAR-T cell therapy and novel immunotherapy combinations are under active investigation in R/R disease

🩸 Staging Dilemma · "Skin-Only" Disease
Isolated skin plaques — is BPDCN ever truly localised?
▾
📋 Clinical Vignette
A 62-year-old man presents with 3 purple-brown skin plaques on the trunk. Skin biopsy: BPDCN confirmed (TCF4+, CD123+, CD303+, TCL1+). FBC normal. The GP refers him to haematology with a letter saying "localised skin BPDCN — can this be managed with radiotherapy alone?"

The haematology registrar is considering whether to perform a bone marrow biopsy and LP.
Q1. Is "skin-only" BPDCN a reliable clinical entity? What staging is mandated?
✓ Expert Explanation
The concept of "skin-only BPDCN" is a clinical trap. While 70–90% of patients present with skin lesions, bone marrow involvement ultimately occurs in 50–90% of cases — and critically, early bone marrow infiltration may be morphologically subtle and only detectable by sensitive immunohistochemistry or flow cytometry. A normal FBC does not exclude low-level bone marrow infiltration. Clinical features section

Mandatory staging for all BPDCN: (1) Bilateral bone marrow trephine biopsies with IHC including CD123/TCF4/TCL1, (2) PET-CT, (3) CSF examination with flow cytometry, (4) LDH, β2-microglobulin. The biological rationale: BPDCN is a systemic disease in which skin lesions represent the most visible manifestation of a disseminated process. Radiotherapy to skin lesions alone would be inadequate therapy even for apparently "localised" presentations.
Q2. The patient's bone marrow biopsy shows 8% CD123+/TCF4+ cells on IHC but bone marrow blast % appears normal on H&E alone. What does this mean?
✓ Expert Explanation
H&E morphology alone is profoundly insensitive for BPDCN marrow assessment. BPDCN cells in the bone marrow can be scattered interstitially or in small clusters, with morphology that easily blends with haematopoietic precursors. IHC with CD123, TCF4, or TCL1 dramatically increases detection sensitivity.

Even 8% involvement confirmed by IHC constitutes bone marrow disease — staging as systemic BPDCN. This patient should not receive local radiotherapy as primary treatment; systemic therapy (tagraxofusp) with allo-SCT consolidation is the appropriate pathway. This case illustrates why all BPDCN bone marrow biopsies must have a dedicated BPDCN IHC panel regardless of H&E appearance.
★ Key Teaching Points
"Skin-only BPDCN" is almost always a staging artefact — 50–90% have bone marrow involvement detectable by IHC even with normal FBC
H&E alone is insufficient for bone marrow BPDCN assessment — CD123, TCF4, and TCL1 IHC must be performed on all trephines
BPDCN is a systemic disease; local radiotherapy to skin lesions is appropriate only as palliation, never as definitive monotherapy
All BPDCN staging must include bilateral BM trephines, PET-CT, and CSF flow cytometry regardless of initial clinical presentation
