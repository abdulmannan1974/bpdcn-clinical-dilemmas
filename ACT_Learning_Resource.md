### PHASE 2: FOUNDATIONS (Sessions 1-2)

#### Session 1: Definition, History, and Principles

**Core Concepts to Understand:**

1. **What is ACT?**
   Activated Clotting Time is a point-of-care whole blood coagulation test that measures the time (in seconds) from blood-reagent contact to clot formation. Originally developed in 1966 by Hattersley and Hayse for monitoring UFH (unfractionated heparin) anticoagulation during cardiac surgery.

2. **Why a Different Test?**
   aPTT is performed on citrated plasma and is affected by:
   - Platelet count and function
   - Dilutional effects during CPB (cardiopulmonary bypass)
   - Hypothermia

   ACT uses **whole blood** (not plasma), making it ideal for the OR and ICU environment where rapid turnaround and resistance to haemodilution are critical.

3. **Clinical Timeline:**
   - 1966: Hattersley & Hayse develop ACT
   - 1970s-1980s: Widely adopted in cardiac surgery for UFH monitoring
   - 1990s: Introduction of heparinase-modified ACT to exclude heparin effect
   - 2000s: Expansion to ECMO, dialysis, and interventional cardiology
   - Current: Routine use in CPB but declining in some ICU settings due to non-specificity

**Key Resources:**
- BSH Guidelines on Perioperative Haemostasis (2016)
- ASH Guidelines on Anticoagulation Monitoring
- Practical Haemostasis (practical-haemostasis.com)

#### Session 2: Methodology and Technical Principles

**How Does ACT Work?**

1. **The Reagent:**
   ACT uses celite (diatomaceous earth), glass beads, or kaolin as **activators**. These activate the intrinsic coagulation pathway much faster than physiological contact activation. Common ACT reagents:
   - Westergren tube method (original, rarely used now)
   - Automated analysers (Medtronic ACT Plus, Sorin Hemochron, Abbott iSTAT)

2. **The Principle:**
   - Patient's **whole blood** (not plasma) added to tube with activator
   - As clotting cascade proceeds, fibrin formation changes blood viscosity/impedance
   - Detector (mechanical/optical) senses clot formation
   - Time from addition to clot detection = **ACT (seconds)**

3. **Clinical Target Ranges:**
   - **Baseline (before heparin):** 80–120 seconds
   - **CPB target:** 400–600 seconds (different surgeons prefer different ranges)
   - **ECMO target:** 220–260 seconds (less intensive anticoagulation than CPB)
   - **Haemodialysis:** 150–200 seconds

**Why Whole Blood?**
- Platelet aggregation contributes to clot formation
- Shear forces and blood cells interact naturally
- Faster result (5–10 minutes vs. 30+ minutes for aPTT)
- Less susceptible to haemodilution effects than plasma-based tests

### PHASE 3: CLINICAL APPLICATION (Sessions 3-4)

#### Session 3: ACT in Cardiac Surgery (CPB Monitoring)

**The CPB Scenario:**

When a patient undergoes cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass:
1. The patient is anticoagulated with **IV UFH** (goal: prevent thromboembolism during CPB)
2. ACT is checked **at baseline**, **immediately after heparin bolus**, and **every 15-30 minutes during CPB**
3. Additional heparin is given if ACT falls below the target (typically 400–600 seconds)
4. At the end of CPB, **protamine** is given to reverse heparin
5. ACT is rechecked to confirm adequate reversal

**Clinical Decision-Making:**

- **ACT too low** (< 400s during CPB):
  ➜ Patient at risk of thromboembolism and graft thrombosis
  ➜ Action: Give additional UFH bolus

- **ACT too high** (> 600s):
  ➜ Increased bleeding risk, prolonged surgical time
  ➜ Action: Reduce heparin infusion or use other tests (platelet aggregation) to guide transfusion

- **ACT not responding to heparin:**
  ➜ Consider: insufficient heparin dose, heparin resistance, dilutional coagulopathy, or hypothermia
  ➜ Investigate: Check platelet count, fibrinogen, FBC; warm the patient if hypothermic

#### Session 4: ACT in ECMO and Non-CPB Settings

**ECMO (Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation):**

ECMO is used for severe respiratory or cardiac failure. Unlike CPB, ECMO:
- Runs for **hours to weeks** (vs. CPB: minutes to hours)
- Requires **less intensive anticoagulation** because blood flow is slow and patient is already anticoagulated
- Target ACT: **220–260 seconds** (much lower than CPB)
- Rationale: Lower target reduces bleeding without sacrificing circuit thrombosis prevention

**Other Point-of-Care ACT Settings:**

1. **Interventional Cardiology:** ACT target 250–350s during coronary angioplasty
2. **Haemodialysis:** ACT 150–200s if anticoagulation required
3. **Apheresis:** ACT 150–180s
4. **Trauma/Massive Transfusion:** ACT used as part of viscoelastic testing (TEG, ROTEM)

### PHASE 4: ADVANCED / EXAM-LEVEL (Sessions 5-6)

#### Session 5: Variables Affecting ACT and Special Modifications

**Variables That Prolong ACT (mimic heparin effect):**

| Variable | Mechanism | Clinical Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Platelet count < 50 × 10⁹/L** | Fewer platelets → slower thrombin generation | Crit: bleeding risk already high; ACT unreliable |
| **Fibrinogen < 100 mg/dL** | Insufficient substrate for thrombin → slower clotting | Crit: massive transfusion scenario |
| **Hypothermia (< 32°C)** | Enzyme kinetics slow; platelet dysfunction | Crit: trauma, post-CPB |
| **Haemodilution (haematocrit < 20%)** | Fewer coagulation factors per unit blood | Crit: CPB, post-operative anaemia |
| **Platelet dysfunction** | Impaired aggregation despite normal count | Crit: post-CPB thrombocytopathy, aspirin use |
| **Coagulation factor deficiency** | (II, V, VII, VIII, IX, X, XI, XII) | Crit: post-CPB consumption; DIC |

**Variables That Shorten ACT (mimic heparin reversal):**

- Hypercalcaemia (enhances thrombin activity)
- Elevated temperature (increases enzyme kinetics)
- High haematocrit (increased coagulation factors per unit)

**ACT Modifications:**

| Modification | What It Does | When Used |
|---|---|---|
| **Heparinase-Modified ACT** | Uses heparinase enzyme to inactivate heparin *in vitro* | Distinguish true coagulopathy from heparin effect post-CPB |
| **Xa-ACT** | Directly activates Factor X, bypassing intrinsic pathway | Monitors LMWH (enoxaparin) and direct Xa inhibitors (apixaban) |
| **MAX-ACT** | Modified celite reagent; less affected by platelet dysfunction | Attempted refinement; limited clinical adoption |
| **Plasma ACT** | Uses platelet-poor plasma instead of whole blood | Research tool; not routine |

**Heparinase-Modified ACT in Practice:**

Draw two ACT tubes:
1. **Standard ACT** (celite-activated whole blood)
2. **Heparinase-modified ACT** (same, but heparinase inactivates UFH in tube before testing)

**Interpretation:**
- If both ACTs are prolonged: coagulopathy present (not just heparin)
- If standard ACT prolonged, heparinase ACT normal: heparin effect only
- Helps guide post-CPB transfusion decisions and detect DIC

#### Session 6: Evidence-Based Interpretation and Guideline Summary

**Interpretation Framework:**

```
PATIENT ON UFH, ACT MEASURED:

1. Is ACT BASELINE appropriate (80–120s)?
   YES → Check heparin dosing
   NO → Investigate baseline prolongation (DIC? thrombophilia medication?)

2. After heparin bolus: Is ACT in target range?
   YES → Proceed
   NO → Check patient factors (weight, renal function, heparin dose)

3. During infusion: Is ACT stable?
   YES → Continue monitoring per protocol
   NO → Check for:
        - Heparin resistance (rare; requires direct thrombin inhibitor)
        - Dilutional coagulopathy (massive transfusion)
        - Hypothermia (warm patient)
        - New anticoagulant started (DOACs, warfarin)?

4. Post-intervention: Is ACT normalizing after protamine?
   YES → Heparin adequately reversed; assess bleeding
   NO → Consider:
        - Inadequate protamine dose
        - Continued heparin effect (rebound)
        - Underlying coagulopathy (draw fibrinogen, PT, APTT, platelet count)

5. Unusual ACT result? Draw HEPARINASE-MODIFIED ACT
   - Distinguish heparin effect from true coagulopathy
   - Guide transfusion decisions
```

**Key Evidence and Guidelines:**

- **BSH Perioperative Haemostasis Guidelines (2016):**
  ACT is the gold standard for UFH monitoring in the OR. After CPB, heparinase-modified ACT helps distinguish heparin-induced prolongation from consumptive coagulopathy.

- **ASH Guidelines:**
  ACT should NOT be used to monitor DOACs or thrombin inhibitors (use PT, aPTT, or specific assays instead).

- **ISTH Guidance on Point-of-Care Testing:**
  ACT offers speed but is non-specific. In complex coagulopathy, combine with conventional coagulation screens (PT, APPT, fibrinogen, D-dimer, TEG/ROTEM).

**Clinical Pearls (Exam Favourites):**

1. **ACT is whole blood; aPTT is plasma** → ACT faster, less dilution-sensitive
2. **Heparinase-modified ACT is essential post-CPB** to separate heparin rebound from DIC
3. **ACT does not monitor DOACs** → use PT or DOAC-specific assays
4. **Hypothermia, low Hct, low fibrinogen all prolong ACT** → always interpret in clinical context
5. **Heparin resistance is rare** → always rule out other variables first

## PHASE 5: PROGRESSIVE CASE-BASED LEARNING JOURNEYS

### Case 1: Elective Coronary Artery Bypass Graft (CABG) – Routine Scenario

**Station 1: Presentation**

- **Patient:** 68-year-old male, BMI 28, weight 82 kg
- **Diagnosis:** 3-vessel coronary artery disease
- **Plan:** Elective CABG with CPB
- **Baseline Investigations:**
  Hb 14.2 g/dL, Plt 245 × 10⁹/L, PT 12s (INR 1.0), aPTT 28s, Fibrinogen 350 mg/dL
- **Baseline ACT:** 95 seconds (normal)

**Teaching Pause 1 – Pre-CPB Planning**

*Question:* What is your UFH dosing strategy and target ACT for this patient?

*Why This Matters:* UFH dosing must be weight-adjusted. ACT targets in CABG typically 400–600s, but higher targets (500–600s) are used by many surgeons to reduce perioperative thrombosis risk in patients with significant atherosclerotic disease.

*Model Answer:*
Standard UFH bolus: 300–400 units/kg IV. For this 82 kg patient:
- Bolus: 300–400 × 82 = **24,600–32,800 units** (round to ~25,000–30,000 units)
- Post-bolus ACT target: **400–600 seconds** (many surgeons target 450–550s in complex CAD)
- Maintenance infusion: adjust per institutional protocol to maintain ACT target

*Common Mistake:* Using a fixed heparin dose (e.g., always 5,000 units) regardless of patient weight. Obese patients and those with recent heparin exposure may require higher doses; conversely, underweight or elderly patients may have heparin resistance.

---

**Station 2: Heparin Response and CPB Initiation**

- Post-UFH bolus (25,000 units IV): **ACT 450 seconds** ✓ In range
- CPB initiated; ACT checked at 15-min intervals:
  - T=15 min: ACT 480s ✓
  - T=30 min: ACT 420s → within range
  - T=60 min: ACT 480s ✓
- **Aortic cross-clamp time:** 75 minutes
- **CPB time:** 110 minutes (including rewarming)

**Teaching Pause 2 – Monitoring During CPB**

*Question:* At T=30 min, ACT dropped to 420s. What factors on CPB might explain this decline, and would you intervene?

*Why This Matters:* Recognising physiological ACT fluctuations vs. true heparin insufficiency prevents both under-anticoagulation (thrombosis) and over-anticoagulation (bleeding).

*Model Answer:*
Factors causing ACT decline during CPB:
- **Haemodilution** → coagulation factors diluted; ACT should increase if heparin effect intact (paradox: heparin effect amplified)
- **Ongoing heparin consumption** → decreased free heparin; true decline in anticoagulation
- **Hypothermia (if cooled)** → prolonged ACT; rewarming shortens it
- **Platelet count drop** during CPB (normal: 50–70% decrease) → theoretically prolongs ACT but usually masked by heparin effect

*Clinical Approach:*
ACT 420s is still acceptable (> 400s). No intervention needed at this point. However:
- Check **platelet count** (expected: ~70–100 × 10⁹/L; not needed for ACT decision unless very low)
- If ACT had fallen to < 400s, give **additional UFH bolus** (50–100 units/kg = 4,100–8,200 units)

*Common Mistake:* Aggressively increasing heparin dose every time ACT fluctuates. Small variations (450 → 420s) are normal and do not require intervention.

---

**Station 3: End of CPB and Protamine Reversal**

- CPB weaning begun; patient rewarmed to 36.5°C
- CPB time: 110 minutes
- On bypass: UFH used = **25,000 units + additional boluses (total ~28,000 units)**
- Aortic cross-clamp removed; patient off pump
- Pre-protamine ACT: 520 seconds ✓
- **Protamine dose:** 1 mg per 100 units of UFH used = **280 mg protamine IV**
- Post-protamine ACT at 5 minutes: **150 seconds** (elevated; heparin rebound suspected)

**Teaching Pause 3 – Post-Protamine Interpretation**

*Question:* ACT 150s post-protamine is higher than your pre-operative baseline (95s). What does this tell you, and what's your next step?

*Why This Matters:* Not all elevated post-protamine ACT represents pathological coagulopathy. Heparin rebound (recirculation of heparin from tissues) is common and usually self-limiting.

*Model Answer:*
ACT 150s is **above baseline (95s) but below CPB target (400s)**, suggesting:
1. **Adequate protamine effect** (ACT came down from 520s to 150s)
2. **Possible heparin rebound** (residual circulating heparin)

*Next Step:*
- Draw **heparinase-modified ACT** immediately
  - If heparinase ACT is normal (90–110s): heparin rebound confirmed; reassuring; recheck in 15–30 min
  - If heparinase ACT is also prolonged (> 120s): true coagulopathy present; investigate (fibrinogen, PT, aPTT, platelet count)
- Assess **clinical bleeding:**
  - Chest tube output (should be < 1 mL/kg/hour)
  - Operative field (should be dry)
  - If excessive bleeding despite normal heparinase ACT: consider transfusion of FFP, platelets, or cryoprecipitate

*Common Mistake:* Giving additional protamine for heparin rebound without drawing heparinase ACT first. Excess protamine causes thrombosis and is difficult to reverse.

---

**Station 4: Post-operative Course**

- Heparinase-modified ACT: 105 seconds (normal) → **heparin rebound confirmed; reassuring**
- Chest tube output: 150 mL in first hour, then 40–60 mL/hour (acceptable)
- Post-op Day 1: patient discharged to ICU for monitoring
- Repeat ACT at 4 hours: 110 seconds (normalising)
- No further intervention needed; anticoagulation management continues with warfarin per cardiac surgery protocol

**Case Summary:**

| Element | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Diagnosis** | 3-vessel CAD; elective CABG |
| **Key Management Steps** | Weight-based UFH dosing; ACT target 400–600s; heparinase-modified ACT post-CPB; protamine reversal per UFH dose |
| **Guideline Reference** | BSH Perioperative Haemostasis Guidelines 2016 |
| **Exam Angle** | FRCPath Part 2: Recognising heparin rebound vs. true coagulopathy post-CPB; appropriate use of heparinase-modified ACT; weight-based anticoagulation dosing |

**3 Clinical Pearls from This Case:**

1. **Heparinase-modified ACT is essential post-CPB** to separate heparin rebound (expected, reassuring) from DIC/consumptive coagulopathy (requires intervention).
2. **Heparin rebound is normal** after protamine reversal; it is self-limiting and usually does not require additional treatment unless accompanied by excessive bleeding.
3. **Weight-based UFH dosing** is superior to fixed dosing; always calculate bolus and maintenance infusion based on patient weight.

### Case 2: Emergency ECMO for Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)

**Station 1: Presentation**

- **Patient:** 52-year-old female, weight 70 kg, admitted with severe COVID-19 pneumonia
- **Presentation:** Intubated, on maximal ventilatory support; PaO2/FiO2 ratio 45 (severe ARDS)
- **Decision:** ECMO cannulation for respiratory support
- **Baseline Labs:**
  Hb 11.8 g/dL (anaemia), Plt 145 × 10⁹/L, PT 14s (INR 1.2), aPTT 32s, Fibrinogen 420 mg/dL, D-dimer 2.8 (elevated; expected in severe COVID)
- **Baseline ACT:** 98 seconds

**Teaching Pause 1 – ECMO Anticoagulation Strategy**

*Question:* ECMO requires different anticoagulation targets than CPB. Why is the ACT target in ECMO lower (220–260s) than in CPB (400–600s), and what risks must you balance?

*Why This Matters:* ECMO runs for hours to weeks with slow blood flow and venovenous (V-V) or venoarterial (V-A) configuration. Over-anticoagulation increases bleeding risk without additional thrombosis prevention; under-anticoagulation increases circuit clotting and patient thromboembolism.

*Model Answer:*
**ACT target in ECMO:** 220–260 seconds (many centres: 200–250s)

**Why lower than CPB?**
- CPB: high-flow, short duration (< 4 hours); haemodilution and high shear stress demand intensive anticoagulation
- ECMO: slower flow, longer duration (days–weeks); less shear stress; patient already has pro-thrombotic state (sepsis, immobility)
- Risk of bleeding vs. clotting: ECMO bleeding is harder to manage (ongoing transfusions) than CPB; protamine reversibility is limited with ECMO heparin exposure

**Initial UFH strategy for this 70 kg patient:**
- Bolus: 300 units/kg = **21,000 units IV**
- Post-bolus ACT target: **220–260 seconds**
- Maintenance: UFH infusion, goal ACT 220–260s every 4–6 hours during ECMO run

*Common Mistake:* Extrapolating CPB anticoagulation targets to ECMO, resulting in over-anticoagulation and catastrophic bleeding.

---

**Station 2: ECMO Initiation and Heparin Response**

- UFH bolus (21,000 units): **ACT 240 seconds** ✓ In target
- ECMO circuit primed and cannulation successful
- ECMO flow: 4.5 L/min (adequate for 70 kg patient)
- Post-initiation ACT checks:
  - T=1 hour: ACT 235s ✓
  - T=4 hours: ACT 210s ⚠ Below target
  - T=8 hours: ACT 185s ⚠⚠ Significantly below target

**Teaching Pause 2 – Declining ACT on ECMO**

*Question:* ACT has declined from 240s to 185s over 8 hours. List potential causes and your management plan.

*Why This Matters:* ACT decline on ECMO can signal heparin consumption, increased fibrin generation, or inadequate anticoagulation. Left unchecked, it risks circuit thrombosis and patient thromboembolism.

*Model Answer:*
**Causes of ACT decline on ECMO:**

| Cause | Evidence | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Heparin consumption** | Declining ACT despite UFH infusion | Increase UFH infusion rate; recheck ACT in 2–4 hours |
| **Increased fibrin generation** | D-dimer ↑, fibrinogen ↓, platelet count ↓ | Check fibrinogen (cryoprecipitate if < 150 mg/dL); check platelet count; consider TEG/ROTEM |
| **Sepsis/DIC** | New fever, lactate ↑, BP ↓, bleeding | Full sepsis workup; consider adding direct thrombin inhibitor (DTI) if heparin resistance |
| **Inadequate anticoagulation** | No clinical change; ACT naturally declining | Increase UFH infusion (most common) |
| **Laboratory artifact** | Clotted sample, citrate imbalance | Redraw ACT specimen; ensure proper tube fill ratio |

**Management Plan for This Patient:**

1. **Redraw ACT** to confirm result (rule out clotted sample)
2. **Check full coagulation screen:** PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer, platelet count
3. **Assess for DIC:**
   - If fibrinogen < 150 mg/dL: give cryoprecipitate (10 units = ~2 g fibrinogen)
   - If platelet count < 50 × 10⁹/L: transfuse platelets
4. **Increase UFH infusion** incrementally (e.g., increase rate by 500 units/hour)
5. **Recheck ACT in 2–4 hours**
6. **If ACT remains < 200s despite UFH increase:** suspect heparin resistance; consider switching to argatroban (direct thrombin inhibitor) or desirudin (direct thrombin inhibitor); consult haemostasis specialist

*Common Mistake:* Ignoring declining ACT and assuming it will stabilise. Early intervention prevents circuit thrombosis and patient thromboembolism.

---

**Station 3: ECMO Complication – Circuit Thrombosis**

- Despite UFH adjustment, patient remains difficult to anticoagulate
- T=18 hours: ACT 195s (persistent below target despite UFH increase to 40,000 units/day)
- Bedside examination: ECMO circuit appears darkened; blood flow alarm sounding
- Urgent imaging (bedside ultrasound): **Circuit thrombosis suspected**

**Teaching Pause 3 – Managing Heparin Resistance and Circuit Thrombosis**

*Question:* What is heparin resistance, why might it occur in this septic ECMO patient, and what are your therapeutic options?

*Why This Matters:* Heparin resistance in ECMO, if unrecognised, leads to circuit thrombosis, loss of ECMO support, and potential patient death. Recognition and rapid switch to alternative anticoagulation is critical.

*Model Answer:*
**Heparin Resistance Definition:**
Failure to achieve/maintain target ACT despite UFH doses > 35,000–40,000 units/day (or aPTT < 1.5 × baseline despite high UFH).

**Causes in Septic ECMO Patients:**

1. **Antithrombin (AT) deficiency** (most common)
   - Sepsis → consumptive loss of AT (cofactor for heparin action)
   - Diagnosis: AT level < 50% (normal > 80%)
   - Treatment: AT concentrate (Ati, Thrombate III) 1000–1500 units

2. **Platelet dysfunction / Thrombocytosis**
   - Sepsis, inflammation → increased platelet activation
   - Diagnosis: platelet count often normal/high, but function impaired
   - Treatment: supportive (improve sepsis control); consider acetylsalicylic acid if not bleeding

3. **Thrombin generation**
   - DIC or excessive inflammatory activation
   - Diagnosis: ↓ fibrinogen, ↑ D-dimer, ↓ platelet count
   - Treatment: FFP, cryoprecipitate, platelet transfusion; aggressive sepsis management

4. **Inadequate anticoagulation dosing** (rare in modern ECMO)
   - Missed doses, malabsorption, renal dysfunction
   - Diagnosis: review UFH infusion history
   - Treatment: increase dose appropriately

**Management Plan:**

1. **Stat labs:**
   - Full coagulation screen (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer)
   - Antithrombin level (critical; normal > 80%)
   - Platelet count, fibrin degradation products

2. **If AT < 60%:**
   - Administer **AT concentrate** 1000–1500 units IV
   - Repeat ACT in 30 minutes
   - Repeat AT level and re-dose if still low

3. **If DIC features (low fibrinogen, high D-dimer, low platelets):**
   - FFP 12 mL/kg (~ 840 mL for 70 kg)
   - Cryoprecipitate 10 units (if fibrinogen < 150 mg/dL)
   - Platelet transfusion (if < 50 × 10⁹/L and bleeding)

4. **If heparin resistance persists despite AT repletion:**
   - **Switch to argatroban** (direct thrombin inhibitor)
   - Argatroban bolus: 350 mcg/kg (~24.5 mg IV) over 3–5 minutes
   - Then infusion: 10–25 mcg/kg/min, target aPTT 1.5–3.0 × baseline (or ACT 160–180s if ACT available)
   - Alternative: **Desirudin** (hirudin analog) if available

5. **Urgent ECMO team review:** Risk of circuit thrombosis may necessitate **re-cannulation** and circuit change.

*Common Mistake:* Continuing to escalate UFH doses in heparin resistance without checking AT level. AT supplementation often rapidly restores heparin responsiveness.

---

**Station 4: Recovery and Weaning**

- After AT repletion and switch to argatroban: ACT maintained 220–250s
- Patient's respiratory function gradually improved over 14 days
- ECMO weaned over 3 days and decannulated Day 17
- Discharged to ICU on standard post-operative VTE prophylaxis (LMWH)

**Case Summary:**

| Element | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Diagnosis** | Severe ARDS (COVID-19); V-V ECMO for respiratory support |
| **Key Management Steps** | ACT target 220–260s (lower than CPB); recognise heparin resistance; check AT level; supplement AT or switch to argatroban; manage DIC if present |
| **Guideline Reference** | ECMO Organization Guidelines; BSH Haemostasis in Critical Care |
| **Exam Angle** | FRCPath Part 2: Anticoagulation targets in ECMO; heparin resistance in sepsis; AT deficiency in critical illness; alternative anticoagulation strategies |

**3 Clinical Pearls from This Case:**

1. **ECMO ACT targets are much lower (220–260s) than CPB (400–600s)** due to slower blood flow and longer duration; over-anticoagulation increases bleeding without reducing circuit thrombosis risk.
2. **Heparin resistance in septic ECMO is often due to antithrombin deficiency;** check AT level early and supplement if < 60%.
3. **Circuit thrombosis is a medical emergency;** if ACT falls below target and cannot be corrected with UFH escalation, switch immediately to argatroban (direct thrombin inhibitor).

### Case 3: Unexpected ACT Finding Post-CPB – Diagnostic Puzzle

**Station 1: Presentation**

- **Patient:** 72-year-old male, elective aortic valve replacement
- **PMHx:** Type 2 diabetes, CKD Stage 3 (eGFR 45), on aspirin and atorvastatin
- **Baseline ACT:** 92 seconds
- **UFH bolus:** 28,000 units (350 units/kg × 80 kg)
- **Post-bolus ACT:** 410 seconds ✓ In range
- **CPB time:** 95 minutes
- **Pre-protamine ACT:** 480 seconds ✓
- **Protamine dose:** 280 mg (1 mg per 100 units UFH used, assuming ~28,000 units total)
- **Post-protamine ACT (5 min):** 380 seconds ⚠⚠ **Unexpectedly high** (far above baseline of 92s)

**Teaching Pause 1 – Unexpected Post-Protamine ACT Elevation**

*Question:* Post-protamine ACT is 380s (vs. baseline 92s). Your colleagues are considering additional protamine. What is your interpretation, and what testing would you order before giving more protamine?

*Why This Matters:* Not all prolonged post-protamine ACT reflects inadequate protamine dosing. Protamine excess causes thrombosis; distinguishing true heparin rebound from other causes of ACT prolongation prevents harm.

*Model Answer:*
**Differential Diagnosis of Elevated Post-Protamine ACT (380s):**

| Cause | Mechanism | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Heparin rebound** | Residual heparin from tissues | Expected; benign; self-limiting |
| **Protamine excess** | Anticoagulant effects of excess protamine | Rare unless > 300 mg given; check if gave extra protamine |
| **Underlying coagulopathy** | DIC, fibrin consumption, factor/platelet loss | Check fibrinogen, PT, APTT, platelet count, D-dimer |
| **Platelet dysfunction** | Post-CPB thrombocytopathy (universal) | Platelet count often 50–70% of pre-CPB; function compromised |
| **Residual anticoagulant** | Warfarin, DOAC, direct thrombin inhibitor | Check medication history |

**Diagnostic Approach:**

1. **Draw heparinase-modified ACT immediately**
   - If normal (90–110s): **heparin rebound confirmed** → reassuring, recheck in 15 min
   - If prolonged (> 120s): **true coagulopathy** → proceed to coagulation screen

2. **Full coagulation screen:**
   - PT (to assess vitamin K–dependent factors)
   - aPTT (broad screen for factor deficiency)
   - Fibrinogen (consumed in CPB; target > 150 mg/dL post-op)
   - Platelet count (universal post-CPB thrombocytopenia; target > 50 × 10⁹/L)
   - D-dimer (elevation suggests fibrin generation)

3. **Assess clinical bleeding:**
   - Chest tube output (should be < 1 mL/kg/hour)
   - Operative field

**Decision: Do NOT give additional protamine without heparinase-modified ACT.** Protamine excess causes thrombosis.

---

**Station 2: Diagnostic Test Results**

- **Heparinase-modified ACT:** 95 seconds (normal!) → **Heparin rebound confirmed**
- **PT:** 14s (INR 1.1) ✓ normal
- **aPTT:** 34s ✓ normal (baseline was 28s; slight prolongation expected post-CPB)
- **Fibrinogen:** 180 mg/dL (acceptable; target > 150 mg/dL)
- **Platelet count:** 78 × 10⁹/L (expected post-CPB; 45–50% of pre-CPB baseline of 155)
- **D-dimer:** 1.8 (elevated but expected post-CPB)
- **Chest tube output:** 120 mL in first hour (normal; < 1 mL/kg/hour for 80 kg = < 80 mL/hour acceptable)

**Teaching Pause 2 – Interpretation and Management**

*Question:* Given these results, what is your diagnosis and management?

*Why This Matters:* This case exemplifies post-CPB coagulopathy: heparin rebound (benign) combined with expected thrombocytopenia and normal coagulation factors. Recognising this pattern avoids unnecessary transfusion and protamine.

*Model Answer:*
**Diagnosis:** Post-CPB coagulopathy (expected) with **heparin rebound** (confirmed by heparinase-modified ACT).

**Interpretation Summary:**
- ✓ Standard ACT 380s, heparinase ACT 95s = heparin rebound (not true coagulopathy)
- ✓ PT, aPTT normal = adequate vitamin K–dependent and contact factors
- ✓ Fibrinogen 180 mg/dL = adequate (> 150 mg/dL threshold for transfusion)
- ✓ Platelet count 78 × 10⁹/L = expected post-CPB; above transfusion threshold of 50
- ✓ Chest tube output acceptable = no excessive bleeding

**Management:**
1. **No additional protamine.** Heparin rebound is expected and self-limiting.
2. **Recheck ACT in 15–30 minutes.** It should normalise as heparin is metabolised.
3. **Monitor chest tube output closely.** Transfuse if > 1 mL/kg/hour or > 200 mL/2-hour window.
4. **Reassure the surgical team** that coagulopathy workup is normal; post-CPB heparin rebound does not require intervention.
5. **Continue standard post-op monitoring** (repeat coagulation screen in 4–6 hours if concerns).

---

**Station 3: Follow-up ACT (Confirmation)**

- ACT at 30 minutes post-protamine: **140 seconds** (declining)
- ACT at 60 minutes: **110 seconds** (approaching baseline; heparin rebound resolving)
- Chest tube output stable: 40–50 mL/hour
- Patient transferred to ICU in stable condition

**Case Summary:**

| Element | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Diagnosis** | Post-CPB state; expected heparin rebound |
| **Key Management Steps** | Distinguish heparin rebound from coagulopathy using heparinase-modified ACT; avoid unnecessary protamine; monitor clinical bleeding |
| **Guideline Reference** | BSH Perioperative Haemostasis Guidelines 2016 |
| **Exam Angle** | FRCPath Part 2: Interpretation of post-CPB ACT; role of heparinase-modified ACT; when NOT to intervene |

**3 Clinical Pearls from This Case:**

1. **Heparinase-modified ACT distinguishes heparin rebound (reassuring) from true coagulopathy** (requires intervention); always use it post-CPB if standard ACT is unexpectedly prolonged.
2. **Post-CPB thrombocytopenia and heparin rebound are expected;** they do not require intervention unless accompanied by excessive bleeding or fibrinogen < 100 mg/dL.
3. **"Do no harm" principle applies: avoid unnecessary protamine (thrombosis risk) and unnecessary transfusion (infection risk, volume overload).** Let heparin rebound resolve naturally while monitoring for clinical bleeding.

## PHASE 6: SOCRATIC VIVA SEQUENCES (Exam Practice)

### Sequence 1: Opening Viva on ACT Principles (15 minutes)

**SCENARIO:**
A 65-year-old patient is undergoing elective CABG with CPB. The surgeon asks you to establish and maintain ACT-guided heparin anticoagulation for the case.

---

**VIVA QUESTION 1 – Foundational (Opening):**

*"Tell me about the activated clotting time (ACT). Why is it used in the operating theatre?"*

**MODEL ANSWER:**
"The ACT is a point-of-care whole blood coagulation test that measures the time in seconds from blood-reagent contact to clot formation. It was developed by Hattersley and Hayse in 1966 and is widely used in cardiac surgery for monitoring unfractionated heparin anticoagulation during cardiopulmonary bypass.

Why ACT is preferred over plasma-based tests like aPTT in the OR:
- **Whole blood vs. plasma:** ACT uses whole blood, not citrated plasma. This preserves platelet function and clotting factors in their natural state.
- **Speed:** ACT result is available in 5–10 minutes, compared to 30+ minutes for aPTT.
- **Haemodilution resistance:** ACT is less affected by haemodilution during CPB because whole blood provides a more physiological substrate.
- **No centrifugation needed:** Point-of-care convenience in the OR.

Target ACT for CABG is typically 400–600 seconds, much higher than the baseline of 80–120 seconds, ensuring profound anticoagulation during CPB."

**GOING DEEPER – VIVA QUESTION 2:**

*"How does ACT differ mechanically from aPTT? What are the practical consequences of these differences?"*

**MODEL ANSWER:**
"Mechanical Differences:

1. **Sample preparation:**
   - ACT: Whole blood (citrated) + activator (celite, glass beads, or kaolin)
   - aPTT: Citrated plasma + phospholipid reagent + contact activator

2. **What contributes to clot formation:**
   - ACT: Platelets actively participate; coagulation cascade proceeds in physiological conditions
   - aPTT: Only coagulation factors in plasma; platelets removed; artificial phospholipid used

3. **Detection method:**
   - ACT: Mechanical (viscosity change) or optical (light transmission) detection of fibrin formation
   - aPTT: Optical detection of clot formation in plasma

Practical Consequences:

- **Platelet count effect:**
  - ACT: Prolonged if Plt < 50 × 10⁹/L (lost platelet contribution)
  - aPTT: Not affected by platelet count (plasma only)

- **Haemodilution during CPB:**
  - ACT: Prolonged by haemodilution but still reflects in vivo anticoagulation
  - aPTT: Severely prolonged by haemodilution (factors diluted) → unreliable on CPB

- **Heparin responsiveness:**
  - ACT: ACT is very responsive to UFH; direct proportional relationship
  - aPTT: aPTT is less responsive to UFH at high heparin levels (plateau effect)

Because of these differences, ACT is the gold standard for monitoring UFH during CPB, whereas aPTT is used for UFH monitoring in general wards."

**CLINICAL TWIST – VIVA QUESTION 3:**

*"A patient is baseline resistant to heparin: ACT remains 350 seconds despite a UFH bolus of 400 units/kg. List four mechanisms of heparin resistance and how you would investigate each."*

**MODEL ANSWER:**
"Heparin resistance (defined as failure to achieve target ACT despite UFH > 35,000–40,000 units/day) has four main causes:

| Cause | Mechanism | Investigation | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Antithrombin deficiency** | AT is UFH's cofactor; low AT = poor heparin effect | AT level (target > 80%) | AT concentrate 1000–1500 units IV |
| **Elevated Factor VIII** | F8 elevation (inflammation, stress response) | aPTT baseline, F8:C activity | Often resolves with inflammation control; rarely needs intervention |
| **Platelet dysfunction** | ↑ platelet activation consumes heparin | Platelet count (often > 400 in stress response); platelet aggregometry | ASA if not bleeding; address sepsis/inflammation |
| **Thrombin generation** | Excessive fibrin formation (DIC, sepsis) | Fibrinogen, D-dimer, platelet count | FFP, cryoprecipitate; address underlying condition |

In the OR, the most common cause is **AT deficiency** (especially in patients with sepsis, liver disease, or on long-term warfarin). Check AT level first. If low, supplement and recheck ACT."

**REFLECTION (Summary after Q1–Q3):**
We've established that ACT is a point-of-care whole blood test ideal for UFH monitoring in the OR, with mechanical and practical advantages over aPTT. We understand that ACT failure may reflect heparin resistance, and we have a systematic approach to investigate and correct it.

**KEY TAKE-HOMES FROM VIVA SEQUENCE 1:**
1. ACT uses whole blood and is therefore faster and more haemodilution-resistant than aPTT.
2. Heparin resistance on ACT should trigger investigation for antithrombin deficiency, elevated Factor VIII, platelet dysfunction, or DIC.
3. Always check AT level in heparin-resistant patients; AT supplementation often rapidly restores heparin responsiveness.

### Sequence 2: Heparinase-Modified ACT and Post-CPB Decisions (15 minutes)

**SCENARIO:**
Post-CPB, standard ACT is 200s (elevated vs. baseline 95s), but the surgical team is concerned about ongoing bleeding (chest tube output 150 mL in 1 hour). The surgeon asks: "Should we transfuse? Or is this just heparin rebound?"

---

**VIVA QUESTION 1 – Diagnostic:**

*"How would you use heparinase-modified ACT to answer the surgeon's question?"*

**MODEL ANSWER:**
"I would immediately draw two ACT tubes:

1. **Standard ACT** (celite-activated whole blood): reports ACT result, as usual
2. **Heparinase-modified ACT** (celite + heparinase enzyme): heparinase inactivates UFH *in vitro* before testing

**Interpretation:**
- If standard ACT 200s and heparinase ACT 95–110s (normal):
  → **Heparin rebound confirmed.** This is expected and benign. No additional protamine. Reassure surgeon. Recheck in 15 min; ACT should normalise as heparin is metabolised.

- If standard ACT 200s and heparinase ACT also 180–200s (both elevated):
  → **True coagulopathy.** Not just heparin effect. Investigate with PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, platelets, D-dimer."

**GOING DEEPER – VIVA QUESTION 2:**

*"Chest tube output is 150 mL in 1 hour (patient weight 75 kg). Is this excessive? How does your interpretation of heparinase-modified ACT affect transfusion decisions?"*

**MODEL ANSWER:**
"Chest tube output assessment:
- Threshold: > 1 mL/kg/hour is considered excessive
- This patient: 150 mL/1 hour ÷ 75 kg = **2 mL/kg/hour** → **EXCESSIVE**

However, interpretation depends on the **cause:**

**If heparinase-modified ACT is normal (heparin rebound only):**
- Coagulation function is intact
- Bleeding is likely due to:
  - Surgical ooze (minor vessels, pericardium)
  - Expected post-CPB platelet dysfunction
  - Mild fibrinogen dilution
- Management: **Do NOT immediately transfuse.** Assess for continued bleeding:
  - Recheck chest tube output in next 2 hours
  - If > 1 mL/kg/hour persists: consider platelet transfusion (even if count > 100) because post-CPB platelet function is impaired
  - Consider cryoprecipitate if fibrinogen < 150 mg/dL

**If heparinase-modified ACT is also prolonged (true coagulopathy):**
- Coagulation dysfunction present
- Bleeding reflects consumptive coagulopathy, DIC, or factor deficiency
- Management: **Transfuse guided by coagulation results:**
  - FFP if PT/aPTT prolonged (factor deficiency)
  - Cryoprecipitate if fibrinogen < 100 mg/dL
  - Platelets if count < 50 × 10⁹/L or < 100 with ongoing bleeding

In this scenario, **heparinase-modified ACT guides the decision:** if heparin rebound only, transfuse conservatively (watchful waiting); if true coagulopathy, transfuse based on lab results."

**CLINICAL TWIST – VIVA QUESTION 3:**

*"Heparinase-modified ACT is 95s (normal), confirming heparin rebound. But chest tube output remains 200 mL in the next 2-hour period, averaging 1.3 mL/kg/hour. Coagulation labs: PT 13s, aPTT 30s, fibrinogen 140 mg/dL, platelets 65 × 10⁹/L. What do you do?"*

**MODEL ANSWER:**
"Interpretation:
- Heparin rebound is confirmed (reassuring)
- BUT bleeding persists and is excessive (> 1 mL/kg/hour)
- Coagulation labs are **mostly normal** (PT, aPTT) but fibrinogen is **borderline low** (140 mg/dL; transfusion threshold is typically 100–150 mg/dL)
- Platelets are **45% of baseline** (expected post-CPB thrombocytopenia)

Management:

1. **Transfuse cryoprecipitate** 10 units (contains fibrinogen; will raise by ~40–50 mg/dL per 10 units)
   - Goal: fibrinogen > 150 mg/dL post-transfusion

2. **Transfuse platelets** 1 apheresis unit (~3 × 10¹¹ platelets)
   - Rationale: Post-CPB platelet dysfunction is universal; even though count is > 50, function is impaired. Transfusion may improve haemostasis.
   - Expected increment: platelets should increase by 30–50 × 10⁹/L

3. **Recheck chest tube output in 2 hours.** If bleeding persists despite transfusion:
   - Repeat coagulation labs (fibrinogen, PT, aPTT, platelets)
   - Consider TEG/ROTEM if available (may reveal platelet dysfunction or hyperfibrinolysis not captured by standard tests)
   - Refer to haemostasis team or consider return to OR for surgical exploration (bleeding may be surgical rather than coagulopathic)

This management balances the evidence (heparin rebound is benign) with clinical reality (ongoing significant bleeding) and target coagulation parameters to optimise haemostasis."

**REFLECTION:**
The heparinase-modified ACT is the key diagnostic tool post-CPB. It distinguishes heparin rebound (expected, benign, self-limiting) from true coagulopathy (requires intervention). When ACT is elevated but heparinase-modified ACT is normal, we can confidently reassure the surgeon and transfuse conservatively, monitoring for continued bleeding and addressing specific coagulation defects (low fibrinogen, platelet dysfunction) rather than giving empirical FFP.

**KEY TAKE-HOMES FROM VIVA SEQUENCE 2:**
1. **Heparinase-modified ACT is the gold standard post-CPB test** to distinguish heparin rebound from coagulopathy.
2. **Heparin rebound is benign and self-limiting;** do not give additional protamine or unnecessary transfusions.
3. **When heparin rebound is confirmed but bleeding persists, transfuse targeted components** (cryoprecipitate for low fibrinogen, platelets for dysfunction) rather than empirical FFP.

**CARD 6 – CPB Target ACT**

**Level:** Foundation

**FRONT:**
What is the target ACT range during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB)?

**BACK:**
**CPB target ACT: 400–600 seconds** (most surgeons target 450–550s for complex cases)

**Rationale:**
- Much higher than baseline (4–7× increase)
- Ensures profound anticoagulation during high-flow, short-duration bypass
- Prevents thromboembolism in graft and coronary anastomoses
- Some surgeons (especially aortic surgery) prefer > 500s; others are comfortable at 400s

**Remember:** "**4**00–**6**00 for **C**ardiac **B**ypass" (400–600 CPB)

**CARD 7 – ECMO Target ACT**

**Level:** Foundation

**FRONT:**
Why is the ACT target in ECMO (220–260 seconds) much lower than in CPB (400–600 seconds)?

**BACK:**
**Reason: Risk-benefit balance**
- **CPB:** High-flow, short duration (< 4 hours); intense anticoagulation reduces thromboembolism without proportional bleeding risk
- **ECMO:** Slow flow, long duration (days–weeks); over-anticoagulation increases bleeding without reducing circuit clotting risk
- **ECMO ACT target: 220–260 seconds** balances circuit thrombosis prevention with reducing catastrophic bleeding risk

**Clinical consequence:** ACT > 350s in ECMO = high bleeding risk; ACT < 180s = high circuit clotting risk

**Remember:** "ECMO's **E**asy on the **A**nticoagulation" (200–260s vs. CPB's 400–600s)

**Exam Tip:** Common viva question; test understanding of risk-benefit, not just memorising numbers.

**CARD 8 – UFH Bolus Dosing**

**Example (80 kg patient):**

- High estimate: 400 × 80 = 32,000 units
- Typical order: 25,000–30,000 units IV push

**Post-bolus ACT should reach 400–600s within 5 minutes.**

**Factors affecting heparin response:**
- Weight (directly proportional)
- Recent heparin exposure (heparin resistance)
- Antithrombin level (if low, poor response)
- Temperature (cold ↓ effect)

**CARD 11 – ACT Variables: Thrombocytopenia**

**Level:** Developing

**FRONT:**
How does platelet count affect ACT? At what platelet threshold should you worry?

**BACK:**
**Mechanism:**
Platelets contribute to clot formation in whole blood. Low platelet count → slower clot formation → **prolonged ACT**.

**Thresholds:**
- Plt > 100 × 10⁹/L: minimal ACT effect
- **Plt 50–100 × 10⁹/L:** moderate ACT prolongation (~10–20s)
- **Plt < 50 × 10⁹/L:** significant prolongation; ACT becomes unreliable

**Clinical implication:**
- Post-CPB thrombocytopenia is universal (50–70% decrease)
- **Do NOT interpret prolonged post-CPB ACT in isolation from platelet count**
- Always pair ACT with platelet count; consider heparinase-modified ACT if Plt < 50

**Remember:** "**P**latelets → **P**rolonged **A**CT (if low)"

**Exam Tip:** Examiners ask: "Why is ACT unreliable in severe thrombocytopenia?" Answer: platelets are part of the test substrate.

---

**CARD 12 – ACT Variables: Fibrinogen**

**Level:** Intermediate

**FRONT:**
How does fibrinogen affect ACT? What fibrinogen level is critical?

**BACK:**
**Mechanism:**
Fibrinogen is the substrate for thrombin. Low fibrinogen → slower fibrin deposition → **prolonged ACT**.

**Critical thresholds:**
- Fibrinogen > 150 mg/dL: adequate for haemostasis
- **Fibrinogen 100–150 mg/dL:** coagulopathy present; transfuse cryoprecipitate if bleeding
- **Fibrinogen < 100 mg/dL:** severe deficiency; **always transfuse**

**Post-CPB context:**
- Fibrinogen consumption is expected during CPB
- Haemodilution drops fibrinogen by 30–50%
- If post-CPB fibrinogen < 150 mg/dL and bleeding, give **cryoprecipitate 10 units** (~2g fibrinogen; raises level ~40 mg/dL per 10 units)

**Remember:** "**Fib < 150:** Think **Cryo**; **Fib < 100:** Always **Cryo**"

**Exam Tip:** Know the transfusion thresholds; this is frequently tested.

---

**CARD 13 – ACT Variables: Hypothermia**

**Level:** Developing

**FRONT:**
How does core temperature affect ACT during CPB?

**BACK:**
**Mechanism:**
Cold temperature slows enzyme kinetics (including coagulation factors and thrombin):
- **Mild hypothermia (34–36°C):** ACT mildly prolonged
- **Moderate hypothermia (30–34°C):** ACT significantly prolonged (may not reflect true heparin level)
- **Severe hypothermia (< 30°C):** coagulation virtually halted; ACT very prolonged

**Clinical implication:**
- During CPB cooling: ACT prolongation partly due to temperature, not just heparin
- During rewarming: ACT shortens as temperature rises (independent of heparin metabolism)
- **Always consider temperature when interpreting ACT during CPB**
- Target ACT during hypothermia: still aim for 400–600s, but recognise part of prolongation is due to cold

**Management:**
- Rewarm patient to 36–37°C before assessing "true" ACT post-CPB
- Don't give additional heparin in severe hypothermia (enzyme kinetics will catch up with rewarming)

**Remember:** "**Cold → Clot time ↑** (but may normalise with rewarm)"

**Exam Tip:** Think about the physiology; recognise that ACT is affected by factors beyond anticoagulation.

---

**CARD 14 – Heparin Resistance: Definition and Prevalence**

**Level:** Intermediate

**FRONT:**
Define heparin resistance. What is the typical prevalence, and who is at highest risk?

**BACK:**
**Definition:**
Failure to achieve/maintain target ACT despite **UFH doses > 35,000–40,000 units/day** (or aPTT < 1.5× baseline with high UFH doses).

**Prevalence:**
- **Cardiac surgery:** 0.5–2.4%
- **ICU / sepsis:** 10–30% (higher in critically ill patients)
- **Cancer patients:** 5–10%

**Risk factors:**
1. **Antithrombin (AT) deficiency** — most common cause (sepsis, liver disease, warfarin use)
2. **Elevated Factor VIII** — inflammation, stress response
3. **Platelet dysfunction** — DIC, sepsis, thrombocytosis
4. **Baseline elevation** — rarely, patients are born heparin-resistant

**Remember:** "**A**lways check **A**nti**t**hrombin" (AT first)

**Exam Tip:** Heparin resistance is an exam favourite; know the causes and investigation.

**CARD 15 – Heparin Resistance: Antithrombin Deficiency**

**Level:** Advanced

**FRONT:**
A cardiac patient is heparin-resistant (ACT 320s despite 40,000 units UFH/day). How would you investigate and treat antithrombin deficiency?

**BACK:**
**Investigation:**
- **Measure AT level** (normal > 80%)
- If AT < 60%: antithrombin deficiency confirmed

**Pathophysiology:**
UFH works by binding to antithrombin, which then inactivates thrombin and Factor Xa. **Without AT, heparin has no effect.**

**Causes of AT deficiency:**
1. **Consumption:** sepsis, DIC, trauma, massive transfusion
2. **Impaired synthesis:** liver disease, malnutrition
3. **Loss:** haemodialysis (membrane-induced loss), nephrotic syndrome
4. **Medication:** warfarin (paradoxically depletes AT, especially during loading)

**Treatment:**
- **AT concentrate:** 1000–1500 units IV
- Mechanism: replaces deficient AT
- Expected effect: ACT should normalise within 30 min if AT was the limiting factor
- **Recheck AT level** 1–2 hours post-infusion; target > 80%

**Alternative if AT concentrate unavailable:**
- FFP 12 mL/kg (contains AT but dilutes other factors)
- Less effective than AT concentrate but may improve ACT

**Remember:** "**AT → Act**ivates heparin**; low **AT** = no heparin effect"

**Exam Tip:** FRCPath Part 2: describe the physiology, not just the treatment.

---

**CARD 16 – Protamine Dosing and Reversal**

**Level:** Intermediate

**FRONT:**
How is protamine dose calculated to reverse UFH at the end of CPB?

**BACK:**
**Standard formula:**
**Protamine dose = 1 mg per 100 units of UFH used**

**Example:**
- Total UFH used during CPB: 28,000 units
- Protamine dose: 28,000 ÷ 100 = **280 mg IV**

**Alternative formula (weight-based if estimating UFH):**
- **1 mg/kg protamine** (not commonly used; the UFH-based method is preferred)

**Timing:**
- Give IV, slowly (over 3–5 minutes) to avoid hypotension
- Recheck ACT 5 minutes post-protamine (should be close to baseline)

**Key point:**
- Over-dosing protamine (> 300 mg) causes **anticoagulant effects** and thrombosis
- Excess protamine binds to other clotting factors and heparin, prolonging ACT paradoxically
- If post-protamine ACT is still prolonged, draw **heparinase-modified ACT** before giving more protamine

**Remember:** "**1 mg per 100 units**" or "**1% rule**"

**Exam Tip:** Know the formula; examiners ask it on every cardiac station viva.

**CARD 17 – Post-CPB ACT Interpretation Framework**

**Level:** Advanced

**FRONT:**
Post-CPB standard ACT is 150s (baseline was 90s). Walk through your diagnostic approach.

**BACK:**
**Step 1: Is ACT abnormally prolonged?**
- ACT 150s vs. baseline 90s = mildly elevated but not grossly
- Decide: proceed to heparinase test or recheck in 10 min?
- Threshold: if > 120s above baseline, draw heparinase

**Step 2: Draw heparinase-modified ACT**
- If heparinase-ACT normal (90–110s): **heparin rebound** → no action; recheck in 15 min
- If heparinase-ACT prolonged (> 120s): **true coagulopathy** → proceed to Step 3

**Step 3: Coagulation workup**
- PT (vitamin K–dependent factors)
- aPTT (intrinsic pathway, factors)
- Fibrinogen (target > 150 mg/dL)
- Platelet count (expect 50–70% drop post-CPB)
- D-dimer (elevation suggests fibrin generation)

**Step 4: Assess clinical bleeding**
- Chest tube output (> 1 mL/kg/hour = excessive)
- Operative field appearance
- Systemic signs (hypotension, tachycardia)

**Step 5: Intervene based on labs + bleeding**
- Low fibrinogen + bleeding: cryoprecipitate
- Low platelets + bleeding: platelet transfusion
- Prolonged PT/aPTT + bleeding: FFP
- Heparin rebound alone: watchful waiting

**Remember:** "**Heparinase** first, **labs** second, **bleeding** always"

**Exam Tip:** This is a complete framework; examiners give vivas asking for exactly this sequence.

**CARD 18 – ACT Modifications: Xa-ACT**

**Level:** Intermediate

**FRONT:**
What is Xa-ACT and when would you use it instead of standard ACT?

**BACK:**
**Definition:**
ACT using Factor X directly (bypassing intrinsic pathway) instead of celite activation.

**When used:**
1. **LMWH monitoring** (enoxaparin, dalteparin) — standard ACT is not responsive; Xa-ACT correlates better
2. **Direct Factor Xa inhibitors** (apixaban, rivaroxaban) — standard ACT unreliable
3. **Research settings** — some centres testing alternatives to standard ACT

**Current clinical status:**
- **Not widely adopted** in most institutions
- Standard ACT still used for UFH (remains gold standard)
- Xa-ACT development was attempt to improve monitoring of newer anticoagulants but hasn't replaced PT-based assays for DOACs

**Key point:**
- **Do NOT use standard ACT to monitor DOACs or LMWH** — it will not reflect anticoagulation adequately
- Use anti-Xa assay for LMWH; use PT or DOAC-specific assays for DOACs

**Remember:** "Xa-ACT = X factor **A**nticoagulation **C**lotting **T**ime"

**Exam Tip:** Know that ACT is specific for UFH; other anticoagulants require different monitoring.

**CARD 19 – ACT in Special Populations: Pregnancy**

**Level:** Advanced

**FRONT:**
Why is ACT unreliable in pregnant patients on UFH anticoagulation? How would you monitor anticoagulation in pregnancy?

**BACK:**
**Why ACT is unreliable:**
1. **Elevated Factor VIII** — normal in pregnancy (hormonal effect)
2. **Elevated fibrinogen** — rises 40–50% in pregnancy
3. **Haematocrit changes** — may be low (anaemia) or high (haemoconcentration)
4. **Platelet changes** — may be low (gestational thrombocytopenia)

These changes **narrow the range of reliable ACT interpretation.** Standard post-bolus ACT targets may not apply.

**Monitoring in pregnancy:**
- **Preferred method:** **Anti-Xa assay** (measures actual UFH effect, not affected by clotting factor changes)
- Target anti-Xa: 0.3–0.7 IU/mL (therapeutic range; varies by indication)
- Recheck at 5–7 days and monthly thereafter

**Alternative:** aPTT with pregnancy-adjusted targets (may need extended aPTT ratio compared to non-pregnant patients)

**Remember:** "Pregnant → **A**nti-**X**a (not ACT)"

**Exam Tip:** FRCPath Part 2: always mention pregnancy as a special population for anticoagulation monitoring.

**CARD 20 – ACT Limitations and Clinical Context**

**Level:** Advanced

**FRONT:**
List three major limitations of ACT and explain when you would use alternative coagulation tests (TEG, ROTEM, or conventional labs) instead.

**BACK:**
**Limitation 1: Non-specific**
- ACT only measures time to clot formation
- Does not assess platelet function, clot strength, or fibrinolysis
- Alternative: **TEG/ROTEM** (provides clot kinetics, strength, fibrinolysis)

**Limitation 2: Affected by many variables**
- Platelet count, fibrinogen, temperature, haemodilution, Factor VIII
- Hard to interpret ACT in isolation
- Alternative: **Conventional labs** (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, platelets for specific factor assessment)

**Limitation 3: Not sensitive to newer anticoagulants**
- ACT unreliable for LMWH, DOACs, direct thrombin inhibitors
- Alternative: **Anti-Xa, PT-based assays, aPTT** depending on anticoagulant

**When to use TEG/ROTEM instead of (or alongside) ACT:**
- Complex coagulopathy (sepsis, DIC, massive transfusion)
- Post-CPB bleeding (assess platelet function, fibrinolysis, clot strength)
- Guidance for transfusion strategy (functional assessment, not just time-based)

**When to use conventional labs alongside ACT:**
- Baseline or unexpected ACT result
- Assessment of specific factor deficiency
- Post-CPB coagulopathy (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, platelets)

**Remember:** "**ACT is a gate, not a guide**" — use it as a screening tool, but dive deeper with specific tests.

**Exam Tip:** Show understanding that ACT has limitations; modern practice combines ACT with functional tests and conventional labs.
